1.0 Introduction and Preparation

1.1–1.6 Overview
Purpose, roles and how the assessment is conducted

This Assessor Guide provides the questions and model answers across the Process Safety categories. The answers should be the same for all Upstream Business Units globally. It is used both by the candidate, preparing for their face-to-face interview, and by assessment teams, to ensure consistency of assessment across all Business Units. This Guide is referenced in section 3.4 of the LOFT UBP.

The Global Process Safety modules can be commenced and worked through prior to assuming a designated LOFT role. Assessment of competency in the Global Modules must be completed in a face-to-face assessment, which may take place where the candidate currently works or in the Business Unit where the candidate will be working.

The Asset Specific Process Safety Module Assessor Guide is developed by the Business Unit and should be available to each candidate prior to, or at the start of, their assignment. It covers the same categories as the Global Module but focuses the candidate on Process Safety Risk in the Business Unit, with additional emphasis on the Highest Consequence (HC1/HC2) Scenarios and their Critical Safeguards. The Asset Specific Module must be assessed by an Assessment Team in the Business Unit where the candidate is assigned.

Purpose of the Leadership Assessor Guide

  • Provide a Global assessment tool for new leaders to demonstrate competency in managing process safety and risk.
  • Provide consistency in assessment of personnel in LOFT Process Safety positions across Upstream.
  • Provide a single point of resource material for Assessment Teams and Candidates.

Supervisor's Role

  • Review the Leadership Process Safety Assessor Guides / expectations with the candidate.
  • Based on the individual's previous assignments and work experience, assess the current “gap” for each section.
  • Identify any extra developmental activities that would assist the candidate to improve their depth of understanding. Refer to LOFT UBP for additional guidance.
  • Based on current gaps and recommended developmental activities, identify a target timing for the verification — target should be within 90 days of assuming position.
  • Ensure Risk Approval Authority is delegated until verification of competency has been demonstrated.

Candidate's Role

  • Complete the LOFT Global / Asset Specific Process Safety Modules using the LOFT Task Book and the Assessor Guides.
  • Regularly inform supervisor of progress.
  • Liaise with Supervisor and Assessment team when ready for the verification interview.

Conducting the assessment and demonstration of competency

  • Assessors ask open-ended questions to verify competency for each module. Candidates should be prepared to elaborate and provide details and examples.
  • Assessments should not be simply question and answer; assessors consider the candidate's ability to speak fluidly about the concepts and principles underpinning the question.
  • Where further knowledge or skill development is required, a gap closure plan must be put in place. Any identified gaps should be closed prior to receiving Full Risk Approval Authority.

2.1 Risk Management

Describe the intent of the RAM Process
Risk Assessment and Management — five intents
  • Provide a common framework for identifying and managing risks to people, environment, assets, and reputation.
  • Reduce identified risks to an acceptable level.
  • Prevent or mitigate the undesirable effects of potential events throughout the lifecycle of a facility, from early development through the production phase to life end.
  • Enable management engagement and communication to drive the decision-making process towards risk reduction actions.
  • Communicate risks to impacted personnel.
Describe your role in communicating RAM outcomes and mitigations
Who needs to be informed and how?
  • Personnel who could be directly affected by identified risks must understand the nature of the risk and subsequent follow-up activities.
  • This information should be accessible to and communicated to those involved and affected by the hazards, the Site Manager and site management.

For additional information see Enterprise Risk Standard section 5.3 Risk Management Communication.

What does ALARP mean and what does it accomplish?
Definition and purpose

As Low As is Reasonably Practicable.

  • ALARP is a risk acceptance review process that considers the best use of resources compared to the degree of risk reduction, by asking the question “What else can be done to further reduce the risk?”
  • ALARP is the point at which the resources expended to further reduce the risk are significantly disproportionate to the reduction in risk.
  • Risk can never be eliminated entirely, though it can be reduced through the application of the hierarchy of safeguards. These safeguards prevent the undesired consequences. The ALARP principle arises from the fact that time, effort, and money cannot be spent on the attempt to reduce a risk to zero.
  • Risks should be managed to an acceptable level, subject to appropriate management review and approval. The determination of an acceptable level of risk is made by reviewing the safeguards against recognized and generally accepted standards and practices. Risk acceptance is a judgment call by the Risk Manager.
  • For HC1 and HC2 risks, the requirements established in the ExxonMobil ALARP Process for HC Risks shall be followed.
What is residual risk? Why review it? How often?
Definition, purpose, cadence
  • Residual risk is the level of risk that remains after a risk scenario has been closed and accepted.
  • Residual risk is important since, even though reduced to an acceptable level, it has not been eliminated and remains a part of our operations.
  • With no active risk reduction measures being worked, there is potential to lose sight of these risks if not managed appropriately. Also, additional risk reduction options might be available or desirable that were not available at the time of closure.
  • Risks initially identified as Cat 1 and 2 are subject to the residual risk review process.
  • Risks accepted and closed as Cat 1 are reviewed and approved annually.
  • Risks accepted and closed as Cat 2 or lower should be reviewed at least every 5 years, typically as part of the HAZOP/PHA revalidation process.

For additional information see Enterprise Risk Standard section 2.2.4 Residual Risk Review.

Process for approving a Risk Assessment
Understanding accepted risk and mitigation/timing

After completing the risk assessment, the risk assessment team reviews the results with the Risk Manager. In decision analysis, the Risk Manager does the following:

  • Consider the significance of the risk under study, including its effect on the aggregate risk of the asset.
  • Evaluate the effectiveness and feasibility of the potential risk reduction measures.
  • Decide the course of further action to reduce the risk to an acceptable level (ALARP).

This activity requires considerable judgment, since the risk reduction benefits and cost effectiveness of some potential risk reduction measures may be difficult to quantify. The Risk Manager must also evaluate potential benefits of risk reduction measures against potential implementation risks.

Action when a critical safeguard (HC scenario) fails performance criteria
Immediate steps
  • The safeguard owner informs all affected, including the scenario owner as well as operations, maintenance, and technical teams.
  • The scenario owner and site leadership take immediate action to determine if operations are safe to continue, identify any interconnected safeguards to be managed, and secure approval from the BU leadership team for continued operation.
  • Site teams restore the safeguard as a high priority through the “Break-in Work” process.

For additional information see Enterprise Risk Standard section 2.5.1 Critical Safeguards Management.

Scenario: piping below retirement thickness due to CUI
Options to manage risk

Ensure the following aspects are considered:

  • Interim Mitigations — what mitigations can be put in immediately? Can the line be isolated or run at a lower pressure? Can a temporary repair be installed such as a wrap or clamp? Does the area or piping need to be barricaded off? How are these mitigations communicated to all applicable personnel?
  • Risk Screening — initiate a risk screening to ensure the risk of keeping the line in service is being managed until repair. Ensure appropriate people are included in the risk screening and approve a triggered risk assessment if appropriate.
  • Expedite Repair — evaluate options with Maintenance to expedite repair. Consider what other work can be re-prioritized to allow this to happen.

2.2 Training

Training and Competency UBP / CVPE overview
Objective of the UBP and how Key Positions are defined

CVPE (Competency Verification Procedure Excellence) — Implementation 2025. The CVPE project implements the Training and Competency Verification Upstream Best Practice (UBP) to ensure compliance with corporate standards and maintain operational integrity.

The UBP is a structured and disciplined approach incorporating human performance principles, defining competency expectations, and defining specific training outcomes for ExxonMobil Upstream operated facilities. It provides direction on how to:

  • Train, and verify competency of, field-based Operations and Maintenance (O&M) personnel holding Key Positions.
  • Maintain collective competency and ensure operations integrity.

Training plans are developed to meet competency requirements through Foundational Craft Skill Standards (FCSS), Global Competencies, Unit Defined Competencies, and Facility Specific Training (FST). The project emphasises task analysis to identify training needs, verification programs to ensure competency, and collective competency reviews to ensure adequate staffing.

Key position definition: a key position is one that could have a significant impact on operations integrity (OI) through individual decision-making authority, operational control, or individual actions, including emergency response. (Section 1.2)

For additional information see the Training and Competency UBP.

CAS objective and Key Position
Applies where the work area has not yet completed CVPE

The Competency Assurance Standard (CAS) section applies only where your work area has not completed CVPE and is still using the CAS standard.

  • Ensure defined UOG personnel in key positions have the knowledge and skills to operate and maintain facilities in a safe and effective manner.
  • A key position is one that could have a significant impact on operations integrity (OI) through individual decision-making authority, operational control, or individual actions, including emergency response. (Section 1.2)

For additional information see the CAS Manual.

2.3 Operating Procedures

Define Integrity Critical Procedure & expectations
IC definition and execution
  • An Integrity Critical Procedure is a procedure vital to the prevention or mitigation of a major event, such as an uncontrolled emission, fire, or explosion that poses a serious danger to people, the environment or property.
  • At a minimum the step-by-step Integrity Critical Procedure is taken to the work site and executed as written, confirming each step before proceeding to the next step.
Timeframe for procedure revalidation
IC / Normal / Work Aid cycles

Where Integrity Critical categories are in use (CAS route)

  • Integrity Critical — IC procedures are revalidated within a three-year cycle, consistent with CAS UBP requirements. The validation and revalidation dates are to the calendar date of the last validation.
  • Normal — Normal Procedures are revalidated within a five-year cycle, consistent with CAS UBP requirements.
  • Work Aid — the revalidation cycle for Work Aids is defined by the BU.

Under the Training and Competency UBP (CVPE route)

  • A procedure may be revalidated when it is successfully used to execute the task in the field.
  • Not to exceed 36 months.
Global IC examples
Recommended Integrity Critical procedures
  • Pigging operations (load and launch, receive and unload).
  • Overall facility startup, where the facility consists of more than one pressure vessel.
  • Escape Capsule (deployment and retrieval with people inside).
  • Gas-fired heaters and boilers start up and shut down (excluding automated engineered fail-safe logic).
  • Flare lighting (excluding automated engineered fail-safe logic).
  • Transfer of hydrocarbons or hazardous materials to or from marine vessel operations.
  • Any procedure that includes the following activities:
    • Temporary defeat of a safety device that is not managed by the Work Management System.
    • Steps to address a COP, CAT 1 HAZOP, or CAT 1 Risk Assessment finding.

Applying anything but an IC procedure to these tasks requires an exception approved by the Site Manager.

Deviation approvals
IC vs Normal vs Work Aid

Where Integrity Critical categories are in use (CAS route)

  • IC Procedure — Site Manager, except for operator adjustments to address an abnormal situation during facility start-up. This specific instance can be managed with SLS approval.
  • Normal Procedure — Second Line Supervisor or higher.
  • Work Aid — Front Line Supervisor or higher.

Under the Training and Competency UBP (CVPE route)

  • When a deviation from a procedure step is required, work must stop and documented deviation approval must be obtained from the SLS [or BU defined position].
Level of review for new/revalidated procedures
SME / user / FLS / SLS / TLS / Site Manager

Integrity Critical Procedure (CAS route)

  • An IC Procedure should be validated by an SME and a procedure user, endorsed by FLS / SLS / TLS, and then finally approved by the Site Manager.

Under the Training and Competency UBP (CVPE route)

  • Procedures must be technically verified, site validated, endorsed by the FLS, and approved by the SLS [or BU defined position] before they are authorized for use.
Verify personnel follow procedures
Deviations, revalidation, observations
  • Identify how often deviation requests are being submitted for procedures. Ask during revalidation of the procedure how often changes are being made.
  • Ensure periodic observation of procedure execution in the field is occurring. These observations can also be incorporated into permit assessments where the procedure is part of a permit pack.
Tools/triggers for procedures
Task Analysis, HAZOP, and other triggers

The following are ways that tasks are identified:

  • Task Analysis (for Operating Procedures) and Equipment Strategies (for Maintenance Procedures) are the tools used to identify tasks, to decide if a procedure is required and what the level of procedure might be.
  • HAZOPs and Risk Assessments.
  • Findings from incident investigations.
  • Designation of a task as a critical safeguard in a potential Highest Consequence (HC) scenario.
  • Regulations.
  • Company policies and requirements (for example, OIMS).
  • Assessments and audits.
  • New or modified equipment or processes.
  • Change in operating parameters.
  • Safety Alerts.

Once tasks are identified, they are reviewed using the DIF (Difficulty, Importance, Frequency) calculation to determine if written work instructions are needed.

Scenario: procedures out of date by six months
What do you do?
  • Expedite review/validation of the procedures and ensure approval is in place where required to allow unplanned use of a procedure if necessary.
  • Follow up to understand where the process has broken down to allow procedures to become out of date.
Scenario: IC error at night prevents restart
How to resolve?
  • Ask questions about the error and the nature of it. Review the plan with the field team about the requirement to start up that night.
  • If the plan is still to start up, initiate an Interim Directive to adjust the procedure to enable use before it can be formally re-written and validated.
  • As it is an IC procedure, this Interim Directive process will require Site Manager approval.

2.4 Critical Equipment

FIMS criticality A–D
Definitions & repository
  • Criticality A: equipment or systems vital to preventing or mitigating an event that could result in a Safety, Health, or Environment (SHE) consequence level I or II impact per the Corporate SSH&E Risk Matrix (as defined in TMEE330 Risk Matrix Application Guide).
  • Criticality B: equipment or systems vital to preventing or mitigating an event that could result in a Business consequence level I or II impact.
  • Criticality C: equipment/systems not formally included in the scope of FIMS, which should have a fit-for-risk scaled approach to maintenance and optimization consistent with reliability basics.
  • Criticality D: all other equipment/systems not part of A, B, and C. Classified as non-essential with no scheduled maintenance. These are repaired/restored on a condition basis because of surveillance (general visuals, operator care rounds, or other reveal of failure or degradation) with corrective maintenance following RBWS per the Maintenance Best Practice.

These specifications are stored in Meridium & IPES as the repository for Equipment Strategy.

Flowing a well with sustained casing pressure above limits
Questions & considerations

Has a risk assessment or risk screening been conducted with a Well SME? Specific questions on the risk assessment may include:

  • What is the health of other safeguards for managing the integrity of this well (e.g. casing integrity, SCSSV)?
  • What are the operating limits for the well and the annulus?
  • Have they considered potential de-rating due to corrosion over time?
  • What is the nature of the fluid (e.g. hydrocarbon, corrosive)?
  • What are the required actions if limits are exceeded (e.g. shut-in, reduce flow, bleed down)?
  • Have we communicated the risk to operations personnel and ensured they know their role in managing the risk?
  • What diagnostics have been done to determine the source of the pressure (e.g. SME engagement on relevant failures on similar wells, pressure analysis)?
  • Is it possible to repair the leak? If so, what is the cost vs. benefit in risk reduction, including consideration of time to repair?

Other considerations

  • Have any required regulatory notification(s) been made?
Long Term Temporary Defeat approvals
In place for more than 60 days
  • PIC — approves defeat after 1 shift, for up to 7 days.
  • AM (Operations Manager) — approval required after 7 days, not to exceed 30 days.
  • PM (Site Manager) — approval required after 30 days and again every subsequent 30 days.
  • Temporary defeats extending beyond 60 days must be reviewed, risk assessed if necessary, and the repair/resolution plan captured.
Scenario: gas detector bypass TD
Questions & mitigations

Questions

  • Do we know why the gas detector is faulty?
  • Are there any other nearby detectors that are also out of service?
  • What is the outlook for repair?
  • Is it a critical work area (well bay, toxic gas area, single seal pump, etc.)?

Mitigations

  • Regular monitoring of the area by other means (handheld or a temporary detector).
  • Adequate coverage by remaining gas detectors.
  • Elimination of spark potential or hot work permits in the area until repaired.
Scenario: high-level shutdown TD at day 6
Adequacy questions
  • Confirm current mitigation steps are happening.
  • How is the level in the vessel being maintained? E.g. Level Control Valve (automatic), manual control valve (manual intervention to reduce level), etc.
  • What damage could be incurred if liquid overflow happens?
  • What specific process parameters are being monitored?
  • What is the response to the alarm? Do we initiate a shutdown if the alarm goes off?
  • How is this being communicated across shifts/hitches?
  • What is the repair plan? Has an RBWS been performed for this device?
  • Any engineering support required to assist with the fix or further risk screening?
  • Address if additional controls are needed.

Ownership of close-out

Contact the Process Safety Team to obtain the Critical Safeguard Performance Standard for each Process Safety Scenario and identify equipment tags associated with Critical Safeguards. Compare these tags against the FIMS exception report to determine if there are any items related to the Process Safety Scenarios.
  • The Senior Maintenance Supervisor is responsible for close-out of all items on the FIMS Exception Report.

Designated Critical devices & bypass controls
Examples and extra controls

Asset specific — Hebron / Hibernia.

In general, all Safety Critical Devices have assigned Safety Critical Element (SCE) Performance Standard Criteria which dictate performance requirements, and trigger Incident Reporting within the OIMS 9-1 process if degraded or impaired. This may also trigger Certifying Authority and Regulatory Agency notification.

  • PSVs — where redundant PSVs are installed in parallel, one of the two can be isolated. Cannot operate with both PSVs out of service (isolated, or with failed EHT in winter months) without formal Risk Assessment approval.
  • PSTs — failure will cause a PSD. Cannot operate with these out of service (defeated) without formal Risk Assessment approval.
  • BDVs/SDVs — cannot operate with these out of service, or out of compliance with API 521 blowdown rates, without formal Risk Assessment approval.
  • Fire & Gas Devices — for devices identified in the Critical Safeguard Performance Standards, no 2 devices of the same type (fire or gas) can be out of service in the same Fire Zone for more than 24 hours without a Risk Screening to continue operation.
  • BOP — requires compliance with the established SCE Performance Standard Impairment Criteria. A formal Risk Assessment is required if the system is partially impaired but can perform the minimum critical function as defined in the standard. Otherwise drilling shutdown is required.

2.5 Alarm Management

Two elements of an effective alarm system
Manageable & prioritized
  • An effective alarm system is one where the number of alarms presented to the operator is such that they can readily address them without becoming overloaded by the work associated with them.
  • Alarms are presented in such a way that the important ones are readily identifiable and easily prioritized.
Scenario: 30 alarms/hour nuisance rate
Actions
  • An alarm rate of 30 per hour places the Console Operator in the “Reactive” level of alarm rates. This is below what Best Practice classifies as overloaded (>60), but the alarm rate would become unmanageable during a process upset.
  • Despite the low priority of the alarms individually, the overall impact on Console Operator performance of this base level of nuisance alarms needs to be considered.
  • Review with the FLS and consider increasing the priority of repair, and what potential there is for an approved Temporary Alarm suppression in the interim period.
Define COP & actions on excursions
Four conditions & response steps

A Critical Operating Parameter (COP) must satisfy all four of the following conditions:

  • A process condition, or escalation of such, that could result in loss of containment having an immediate and significant SHE impact (Consequence I).
  • Plausible and credible risk exposure.
  • No preventative automated protective system, or not mitigated to ALARP by existing systems.
  • Manual intervention is the final element of control.

Actions on an excursion outside operating limits

If an Operator identifies an excursion of an operating parameter outside its normal operating limits (between its high and low set points), through alarm indication or rounds in the Central Control Room (CCR), they should:

  • Investigate the cause of the alarm and assess its criticality.
  • Assess the current process value versus the safety shutdown value (HH/LL) and take appropriate action to understand and mitigate the issue.
  • Confirm that there are no safety critical devices (i.e. PSTs, TST) that have been defeated. If there are, remove the defeats to allow executive actions to take place as needed.
  • In no circumstance should the Operator defeat a safety device to prevent a process shutdown.
  • Communicate the alarm and request a field operator to investigate as needed.

Asset specific: review the asset COPs if they exist and verify how risk is mitigated for each.

2.6 Work Management

WMS objective & permitted work activities
Scope of the Permit to Work System

The objective of the Work Management System (WMS) is to confirm that all activities necessary to produce, process, and export oil and gas are performed in accordance with Company and government regulations and industry systems, and to fulfil the OIMS System 6-4 objectives that:

  • Controls are in place for temporarily disarming or deactivating integrity-critical equipment (such as a maintenance bypass of a gas detector).
  • Work permits are executed to protect personnel, equipment, and the environment from mechanical and operational hazards.
  • Operational interfaces at the work site and the impact of shift handover on work activities are appropriately managed.

The PTWS addresses the following work activities

  • Hot work
  • Confined space entry
  • Critical work
  • Energy isolation
  • Temporary defeats of critical devices
LSRA purpose
Worker-controlled safeguards
  • Life Saving Rules and Actions (LSRA) define the most critical and life-saving actions controlled by a worker.
  • These worker actions become key safeguards in eliminating higher potential consequences (i.e. life-altering injuries and significant process safety events).
  • LSRAs provide a corporately consistent approach to identifying and managing activities with higher potential consequences.
Roles: PIC / AA / AO / PH
Responsibilities in the Work Management System

PIC — Person-In-Charge

  • Endorses permits, approves the SIMOPS plan, runs the DWCM, provides 24-hour to 7-day endorsement of Temporary Defeats.
  • Endorses single valve isolation plans; oversight of and compliance with the WMS.

AA — Area Authority

  • Production Supervisor or person designated by the PIC, responsible for work coordination at the facility.
  • Review and authorization of permits, TDs and isolation plans.
  • Ensuring competent individuals are assigned to execute tasks and appropriate tools are available.

AO — Area Operator

  • Performs the field review and issuance of the permit to the PH.
  • Conducts or assigns isolation, temporary defeat and zero energy demonstration responsibilities.
  • Reviews and communicates work site controls and safe working processes to the PH.
  • Identifies how Zero Energy will be demonstrated to the Permit Holder, and demonstrates Zero Energy.
  • Verifies work status and restoration of the equipment or system.

PH — Permit Holder

  • Compiles the permit pack documents and associated hazards and controls for the work scope.
  • Interfaces with the AO and AA.
  • Verifies and secures (lock and tag) the energy Isolation Control Point (if applicable) and witnesses the zero-energy demonstration before accepting the permit.
  • Conducts the pre-task briefing and permit pack review.
  • Ensures START work readiness and reinforces STOP Work Expectations with the team, and promotes usage of LMRA.
  • Verifies and validates identified life-saving safeguards and work site controls are in place and effective.
  • Completes the work and restores the worksite to a clean and hazard-free condition.
  • Leads After Action Reviews (AARs) when planned or warranted.
Non-permitted work list & approval
Examples & OIMS 6-4 Owner

Examples may include, but are not limited to: visual/routine inspections; work inside workshops or living quarters that does not involve critical work activities or confined space entry; sampling from an engineered sample point; potable water sampling and testing; general housekeeping; checking and refilling lube oil; routine operator activities (for example, equipment preparation for maintenance or construction, returning equipment to service, and housekeeping); helicopter operations; refuelling operations.

  • OIMS 6-4 Owners must assess and approve all activities on the non-permitted work list, ensuring that the mitigating controls for each task are appropriate.
SVI — ICC endorsement
Who endorses and under what conditions
  • The PIC must endorse the Isolation Control Certificate (ICC) for work to proceed.
  • The PIC must consult the minimum isolation standard table to determine the required condition (Condition A/B) that must be met for zero energy demonstration.
LTI review frequency & escalation
When to involve the Operations Manager
  • PIC/AA conduct a monthly review of the LTI register to confirm status and forward plans (e.g. repairs scheduled, parts not arrived, equipment not required, physical disconnection or blinding required).
  • Quarterly field assessment to physically check the integrity of all isolation points.
  • LTIs must be assessed by the OMT (Operations / Maintenance / Technical team) and, if deemed to pose a risk to safe and reliable continued operations, must be reported to the Operations Manager on a minimum 6-month interval.

The Operations Manager should help determine

  • Additional controls/mitigations needed for continued operations.
  • Alignment on prioritization for repair and reinstatement.
  • Alignment on the need for temporary or permanent positive isolation/removal (MOC).
DWCM purpose & attendees
SIMOPS risk management

The objective of the Daily Work Coordination Meeting is to review and coordinate interfacing activities and manage the risks involved with concurrent activities and simultaneous operations (SIMOPS) by evaluating the proposed jobs prior to endorsement. New and suspended permits are reviewed. Non-permitted work that could conflict with planned activities is also discussed.

  • Establish status of ongoing activities and review new work permits and non-permitted work.
  • Identify High Risk Activities and ensure these activities have adequate oversight.
  • Review requested and applied Temporary Defeats.
  • Assess SIMOPS, and endorse work permits that do not have SIMOPS or concurrent work conflicts.
  • Review and assign assessments as required.
  • On a monthly basis, review Long Term Isolations.

DWCMs should be attended by the key Work Management stakeholders, specifically the PIC, AA, and functional supervisors (AAF).

Temporary defeat purpose & approval level
Complexity, not just duration
  • The purpose of the temporary defeat process and certificate is to control the defeating and reinstatement of critical devices on equipment that remains in service; to confirm that alternative mitigative or preventative controls are put in place; that appropriate approvals are obtained; that affected personnel are aware of the defeat; and that no other work is underway that requires the critical device or system to be operational.
  • Temporary defeats of critical devices or systems must be approved by the Area Authority. Escalation to a higher management approval will be sought if necessary, due to the magnitude/complexity of the defeat or the duration of the proposed defeat.

Note: TD approval escalation is not solely time based — escalation should occur immediately depending on the complexity of the defeat.

Maximum permit validity
Days
  • 7 days, with no ability to extend past that time.
SIMOPS: definition, tool, evaluation, deviations
Matrix & approvals

SIMOPS are activities that take place at the same time and meet the following conditions:

  • Are in the same area.
  • Directly or indirectly could potentially affect the safe performance of the other activity, e.g. Breaking Containment and Hot Work.

Restrictions are determined using the SIMOPS Matrix, which must be used in conjunction with the SIMOPS restriction tables. The Matrix is a quick reference tool used to indicate if activities can be performed simultaneously; the restriction tables provide guidance on the hazard and mitigation of the SIMOPS activities.

Work activities are evaluated for SIMOPS at the DWCM. Identified SIMOPS issues are managed using the standardized approval process according to the SIMOPS deviation guidelines. AAs and AOs are also required to monitor any break-in activities for SIMOPS implications.

Deviation approvals

  • Level 1 — Site PIC.
  • Level 2 — Operations Manager.
  • Level 3 — Site Manager.
Scenario: breaking containment under SVI
Approvals required
  • Isolation valve leak and integrity test must be performed.
  • The isolation plan and use of an SVI for isolation must be approved by both the Production AA and the PIC.
  • If the flange class of the system is equal to or >300, the Operations AA or competent delegate (independent verifier to the AO) must be at the site to witness the demonstration of zero energy.
  • If the flange class is <300, the Operations AO must be at the site to witness the demonstration of zero energy — it cannot be delegated to the permit holder.
Scenario: control valve removal with no bleeder
Actions and questions
  • The first question is whether the job needs to be conducted that way. What is driving this? Is it a production impact?
  • In considering this work, look at the flange-breaking procedures that would be used and any extra precautions that would be put in place as a result of being unable to demonstrate zero energy.
  • If it is confirmed that the job should go ahead, then a Risk Assessment is required and an acceptable means of isolation must be identified, documented, and approved before Breaking Containment.

Elements to verify

2.7 Management of Change

MOC principles
Risk screen, multi-functional review, PSSR, documentation
  • Changes are evaluated to understand the complete effect on the asset risk profile; if they can result in a potential Cat. 1 or 2 risk scenario, a risk screening under OIMS 2-1 should be conducted.
  • Multi-functional assessments are used to identify the full scope of a change.
  • Actions needed to implement a change are effectively executed and recorded.
  • New facilities are reviewed prior to use to ensure what was built matches the proposed change scope (PSSR).
  • Affected workers are notified when a change is ready to be used, and are appropriately trained ahead of time.
  • Management endorsements and approvals are documented to ensure changes are executed within full view of line management.
  • Comprehensive change records are documented using standardized formats.
  • All impacted drawings and documents are updated to include the impacts of permanent changes.
  • The historical record of changes made at the facility is preserved.
When to consider MOC
Operations, procedures, site standards, facilities, organizations
  • Changes in operations, procedures, site standards, facilities, or organizations must be evaluated and managed to ensure that Operations Integrity risks arising from proposed changes remain at an acceptable level.
  • Changes may include, but are not limited to: Operating Envelope changes, feedstock changes, metallurgical change (process safety), temporary facilities, temporary repairs, and changing equipment that is not “in kind”.
Define RIK with examples
Replacement in Kind

A change that satisfies the original design intent and specification and does not affect any OIMS barrier is considered a Replacement in Kind (RIK). While RIKs are not included within the scope of the management of change process, it is imperative that appropriate personnel evaluate all aspects of a potential RIK to ensure the replacement of the equipment (or components) does not impact other OIMS barriers such as regulatory issues (e.g. emissions permit), procedures, or drawings. If there is potential for other OIMS barriers to be impacted, the change must be managed in accordance with OIMS 7-1 and this PBP.

Examples of Replacement in Kind

  • Replacing a 4-in. Brand X ANSI ball valve with a 4-inch Brand Y ANSI ball valve, assuming Brand Y has identical certifications and flange-to-flange dimensions to Brand X. (If the replacement valve has different flange-to-flange dimensions, piping changes would be required and an MOC would be needed.)
  • Replacing a leaking pipe spool with an exact duplicate (size and materials).

Note: in some cases, using an MOC may be helpful to manage a RIK — for example when replacing a critical valve, in terms of managing associated activities like critical spare parts and associated maintenance procedures.

Temporary change & administrative components
Duration, mitigations, approvals

Temporary changes are interim changes needed until the original configuration can be restored, or a permanent change can be implemented.

Examples

  • Installation of temporary pipe repair patches until the pipe can be replaced.
  • Installation of temporary equipment (including 3rd party equipment) not specifically identified under an approved Company procedure.
  • Temporary process changes which affect alarm limits.

Three additional administrative components

  • An authorized duration for which the change can be used.
  • A process to ensure that mitigating measures associated with the change remain sustained and effective for the approved duration.
  • A process to ensure that approval dates are not exceeded.

If the approved duration for a temporary MOC is extended, a process exists to verify that risks associated with the extended time period are fully understood and appropriately mitigated.

When does an MOC require a PSSR?
Field changes
  • Any change that includes facility changes in the field should have a PSSR (Pre-Startup Safety Review) once construction is completed, before the change is put into service.
  • The PSSR should be completed, and any pre-startup follow-up items completed, before the change is AFU'd (Approval for Use). Post-startup follow-up items can be completed after AFU.
Scenario: weekend CS valve replacing SS
Considerations
  • Are there any SSHE effects in not implementing the requested change to a carbon steel valve?
  • If agreeing to proceed, what mitigations will be put in place to ensure the service does not cause the valve to fail earlier than planned?
  • Can we run on bypass?
  • Is there history of this activity? Were there any issues previously? Have engineering calculations been used to determine corrosion allowances?
  • Should a second valve be ready if corrosion effects are excessive?
Scenario: paperwork missing but pressure to restart
Actions
  • Verify why it is so important to rush the activity; if the driver is financial, slow down the FLS.
  • Establish what documentation is missing, e.g. P&ID, Electrical One-Line drawing, PSSR.
  • Ensure all action items associated with the MOC have been endorsed, allowing equipment to be reinstated.
  • Ensure full understanding of the risk without the equipment; consider increased monitoring of the equipment.
Asset Specific: MOC review & close-out steps
Workflow

Asset specific — Hebron / Hibernia.

  • Walk through an open MOC in the asset's MOC system and be able to describe the review, endorsement, approval and close-out steps, and who holds each.
  • Be prepared to explain how temporary MOC durations are tracked and what happens when an authorized duration is approaching expiry.

2.8 Incident Investigations and Analysis

Notification vs Investigation (Process Safety vs Personnel)
Paths & criteria

Notification — letting others know

  • Determine actual and potential severity of the event and the notification path.
  • Internal — notify appropriate organization levels.
  • External — comply with government/regulatory requirements.

Investigation — what happened and why

  • Use the GO&S incident investigation framework Reference Guide (located at goto/clfi) to guide your investigation.
  • Determine and appoint the appropriate investigation team and scope.
  • Conduct the investigation — determine facts and sequence of events.
  • Determine potential consequence and causal factors.
  • Develop corrective actions (aligned with potential consequence and causal factors).

Level of reporting for both Personnel and Process Safety events depends on both the actual and potential severity of the incident. For injuries and illnesses use the GO&S Hurtfree approach severity guide. For Process Safety this is determined by the IRAT score and what Tier the Process Safety Event is (Tier 1 being the most severe).

Incident investigations, final reports, and corrective actions should be stored in IMPACT.

Verify FLS/SLS understanding & follow-up
Communication, details, verification

Three elements ensure this occurs:

  • Communication — expectations are communicated to all FLS and SLS through a readily available procedure/guide. Ensure the asset's Internal Notification Matrix is understood by users.
  • Details — clear details of notification expectations, contact names and numbers etc. are made available to FLS and SLS and regularly updated as personnel move through different positions.
  • Verification — regular efforts to test that expectations are being followed, through drills, incident reviews and incident investigations that are effective and completed in a timely manner. Action items should be entered, tracked, and closed in IMPACT.
Scenario: IRAT score of 100 seems low
Questions to test accuracy

Before you ask anything, clarify why you want to know — be conscious of the bias it can introduce in your team if you are anxious about the IRAT outcome. Reinforce the desire to accurately capture high-potential incidents and near misses.

  • Talk me through the worksheet.
  • How bad was it? (Actual Consequence)
  • How bad could it have been? (Potential Consequence)
  • How well protected were we? (Barriers Remaining) — safeguards that worked, safeguards that didn't work.
  • Steward each step according to the IRAT guidance on the worksheet.

Ensure the team is mindful that a common error in IRAT is inconsistency between the Potential Consequence and the Barriers/safeguards. The safeguards need to be relevant to the potential consequence — e.g. it may not be appropriate to select your Potential Consequence as a Lost Time Injury and a barrier of Emergency Response; either the Potential Consequence is too low, or the Barrier count is too high.

Where to find notification information
OIMS 9-1 and the Quick Reference Book

Asset specific — Hebron / Hibernia.

  • A Preliminary Incident Notification is sent to the onshore and offshore leadership team with high-level incident details. Notifications are sent to the C-NLOER as required.
  • All requirements can be found in OIMS 9-1 Incident Management (Incident Notification, Investigation and Reporting Process), and can also be referenced in the Incident Management Quick Reference Book.
Action stewardship & incident summaries
SharePoint, morning call, LFI

Asset specific — Hebron / Hibernia.

  • Incident actions are stewarded through the Incident Investigation SharePoint, and the status of actions is stewarded weekly through the Asset Morning Call with Leadership.
  • Contact the Risk and CA advisor for a summary of any Significant Process Safety events in the Asset/BU in the last 12 months (Tier 1, Tier 2, Tier 3 with IRAT 400+).
  • Learning from Incidents: global and local lessons learned are shared in the Monthly Process Safety and Learning from Incidents meeting; applicable actions are stewarded through the Incident Investigation SharePoint. Information is also shared through safety meetings and the platform Daily Communicator, and a platform leadership message is provided to all flights and vessels arriving with crew. All HLVI or HiPo safety bulletins are reviewed and analysed for key root causes, not just discussed at morning call.

2.9 Environmental

Scenario 1: offshore platform within 10 miles of the coast
Impacts & questions

Possible impacts to the environment

  • Acute accidental spill, leak, or overtopping event (oil, produced water, chemicals, diesel etc.).
  • Well control issue.
  • Releases via flare (burnt or unburnt gas, smoke).
  • Greenhouse gases (turbine exhaust, engine exhaust, drilling equipment emissions).

Possible questions to ask

  • What marine life is frequent in the area?

  • What is the history of environmental near misses/incidents on the platform?
  • Is the drilling rig equipped to ESD operations of the production platform and vice versa, should an environmental incident occur?
  • What environmental response capabilities are available should an incident occur?

3.0 Emergency Response

Scenario: Onshore IC — Apply PEAR
People, Environment, Asset, Reputation

Apply PEAR to the briefing: identify at least 3 issues under People and at least 2 under each of Environment, Asset and Reputation. For each, state the objective (what you plan to do) and the strategies (how you plan to accomplish it).

People

  • Ensure safety of site/facility personnel; ensure safety of citizens and responders.
  • Verify personnel accountability; implement mustering procedures (validate headcount, evacuation trigger).
  • Understand and identify hazards of the emitted product or substance; monitor air for designated pollutants in the impacted areas.
  • Establish On-Scene Command at site; implement the site's ERP or TRP.
  • Establish site control (hot, warm and cold zones, and security).
  • Coordinate fire, medical, rescue, and hazmat response actions; determine and resource PPE based on identified hazards.
  • Identify injured personnel and make any necessary notifications; establish a care management process and communication with next of kin based on severity.

Environment

  • Ensure safety and health of the local community; maximize protection of environmentally sensitive areas.
  • Assess possible on- and off-site hazards to the environment; monitor air within the community.
  • Confirm reporting requirements and verify all initial government reporting is completed.
  • Track pollutant movement, develop trajectories or plume models, and identify resources at risk in impacted or potentially impacted areas.
  • Implement pre-designated response strategies.

Asset

  • Establish the EOC and stand up the IMT; implement the site's ERP or TRP.
  • IMT to establish staging areas for response equipment and resources.
  • Assess and inspect facility integrity; initiate the disaster assessment process to inspect facilities for damage or unsafe conditions.
  • Execute the internal notification process (all-call).
  • Focus on controlling the source and assess suppression activation status.
  • Leverage the ERP for mutual aid, local EMS, and oil spill response organizations.

Reputation

  • Issue an approved holding statement; issue internal employee communication.
  • Notify stakeholders; address offsite property damage or losses.
  • Leverage P&GA to develop, route and issue approved statements; leverage P&GA and HR for internal communications.
  • Leverage IMT resources to make notifications; establish a claims process.

Tactical Response Plans and asset process flows
TRP objectives, phases, and asset familiarization

What a TRP is

The Tactical Response Plan outlines initial responding actions to an unplanned but identified emergency scenario, to mitigate escalation and thereby limit potential harm to people and the environment. The audience is process operators and technicians competently trained in process anomalies, familiar with the material substance or product's inherent properties, and qualified in emergency response decision-making actions.

Objectives

  • Provide clear, simple direction for personnel who discover or respond to a potential HC event.
  • Establish an emergency response mentality immediately upon discovery.
  • Drive response mitigation options to monitor and control a potentially serious emergency outcome.

Response phases and their objectives

  • Initial discovery — protect personnel in the immediate area; assess and report initial discovery; determine initial response and minimize escalation potential.
  • Initial response — control or mitigate sources where escalation is imminent; account for personnel; initiate the notification process, support resources and inform stakeholders.
  • Proactive response leading to recovery — understand the developing hazard and looming risks before choosing mitigation or further actions; execute the mitigation plan; maintain formal communication; transition to the recovery organization.
